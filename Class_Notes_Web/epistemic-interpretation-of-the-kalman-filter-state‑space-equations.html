0. Introduction: The State‑Space Foundation

The standard starting point for the theory of Kalman Filtering (KF) rests on representing a dynamic system using state‑space equations. While the mechanical and multibody systems we analyze are governed by physical laws that are fundamentally continuous in time, modern estimation algorithms are executed on digital hardware using sampled data. Therefore, throughout this text, we will exclusively use the discrete‑time formulation.

This state‑space framework is built upon two coupled mathematical models:

  1. The Process Equation: Describes how the internal state of the system evolves from the current time step () to the next (), driven by internal dynamics and external inputs.
  2. The Sensor (Observation) Equation: Describes how those hidden internal states project outward into the measurable quantities recorded by our sensors.

Together, these equations provide the mathematical architecture to track a system’s true trajectory over time. However, a conceptual collision occurs when we transition from pure deterministic mechanics to estimation theory. In filtering, we inject stochastic variables (means, covariances, white noise) into our exact kinematic and dynamic models. If we blindly interpret these statistical terms as purely physical phenomena, we will quickly run into paradoxes—especially when dealing with parameters that are physically constant but simply unknown to us.

The following epistemic (knowledge‑based) dissertation is a necessary philosophical foundation. It bridges the gap between the exact deterministic reality of a physical system and the probabilistic nature of our mathematical models. Internalizing this perspective is critical for correctly grasping the statistical reasoning that will appear throughout the subsequent chapters.

2. The Epistemic Shift (Knowledge View)

While the ensemble view provides a physical basis for probability distributions, we frequently face scenarios where a physical variable is strictly deterministic in reality, but its true value is unknown to us. Leaving aside the realms of quantum and statistical mechanics, this is the realm of classical mechanics that most exactly represents our experience of the physical world as humans.

Instead of conflating physical reality and our knowledge using the exact same mathematical variables, we introduce a strict epistemic shift. We acknowledge the true deterministic reality () but map these variables into an epistemic domain to formulate our belief. We do this by prepending a left superscript to denote the epistemic random variable associated with a specific deterministic magnitude.

5. The Epistemic Flow of State Knowledge

Finally, recognizing the epistemic nature of these equations requires us to explicitly trace the sequential source of our knowledge at each step of the filter.

Because the equations track our evolving belief rather than just physical randomness, as in statistical mechanics, the state estimate represents different stages of inference depending on the equation:

  • In the prediction (process) equation, the source of information for the state is our posterior inference—the updated estimate derived in the previous step (). We project this past knowledge forward leading to the so called prediction ().
  • In the observation (sensor) equation, the source of information for the state is our prior inference—the prediction just generated by the process equation (). In this context the inference for will be referenced as .
  • The prediction and the observation are combined via an “information‑weighted” average to obtain the updated estimation (posterior inference).

6. Conclusion: Reconciling the Views

This reveals the ultimate power of the epistemic formulation: the equations abstract away the origin of the uncertainty, but our notation explicitly tags it. The randomness captured by a covariance matrix can be “real” (e.g., physical wind gusts driving ) or “perceived” (e.g., a deterministic temperature measured imperfectly by ).

By explicitly separating the exact deterministic variable () from our probabilistic representation of it (), the epistemic view serves as the most general framework: it allows us to interpret our predictions as the absolute best estimates available, strictly defined by the limits of our current knowledge.

Observability is a fundamental concept in control theory and state estimation. It is defined as the ability to determine the state of a system at a given step, given measurements in that and following steps. If a system is observable, it means that by watching its inputs and outputs over a period of time, we can mathematically reconstruct its internal state.

3. The Degree of Observability – Epistemic (Stochastic) View

The classical observability matrix provides a fundamental geometric baseline. It dictates whether the measurement axes, propagated through the system dynamics, eventually span the entire state space. This algebraic rank condition is an absolute, non‑negotiable barrier: if , the system contains an unobservable subspace. In such cases, no amount of filtering, smoothing, or sensor fusion can recover the states that remain structurally decoupled from the observations.

However, while full rank () is a strict mathematical prerequisite, it is frequently insufficient for practical implementation. The classical LTI and LTV formulations implicitly assume perfect state transitions and exact sensor mappings. In complex mechanical systems—such as multibody dynamics subject to unmodeled joint compliances, friction, or rigid‑body approximations—this binary view fails to capture the physical reality of the estimation problem.

To transition from the ontological question of “Is it observable?” to the epistemic question of “To what degree is it observable?”, we must fully reintroduce the stochastic nature of our models.

In practice, this renders the state functionally unobservable, buried beneath the noise floor.

3.3. Trajectory Observability vs. Equilibrium Linearisation

A critical limitation of classical linear theory arises when a nonlinear system is evaluated strictly by linearising it around a single, stable equilibrium point. At this fixed state, the Jacobians (, ) remain constant. If the resulting classical observability matrix is rank‑deficient at this point, linear theory strictly declares the system unobservable. However, this conclusion represents a “linearisation trap”—it is only true if the system never actually leaves that exact equilibrium state.

In the physical reality of a nonlinear system, as the state moves along a dynamic trajectory away from equilibrium, the Jacobians change continuously. This continuous evolution gives the system “richness,” effectively rotating the measurement axes within the state space. By scanning the state space from these changing geometric perspectives, the varying Jacobians often couple previously hidden dimensions into the sensor outputs, rendering the system observable in motion even if it was unobservable at rest.

The Stochastic Information Gramian naturally captures and quantifies this phenomenon because it evaluates observability over a dynamic trajectory rather than at a static tangency. Because the matrices inside this summation are evaluated at varying states along the trajectory, each term in the sum represents a different geometric projection. Even if an individual term at a specific step is algebraically rank‑deficient, a subsequent term evaluated at a different state may possess a row space that covers the previously missing dimensions. Consequently, the aggregated sum can reliably build up to full rank (), mathematically demonstrating how the rich dynamics of the trajectory render the complete state space practically observable.
